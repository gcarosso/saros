Why the sector eight arguments, each with the evidence behind it

1 · It has paid, in aggregate

For the twenty years before 2020 the S&P Biotech Index returned 9.3% a year against 6.1% for the S&P 500 and 7.6% for the Russell 2000. The return comes from a few acorns that grow into oaks; they pay for a morass of failures (see the cautions below).

2 · It diversifies a book

Because outcomes hinge on science and on big-pharma acquisitions rather than the business cycle, biotech's average correlation with every other sector is 0.32, below any other industry pair. In portfolio terms, that is the scarcest property a sector can have.

3 · Names inside it do not move together

Average pairwise correlation of 12-month returns within the sector is 0.25, second-lowest of twelve. Diversification inside biotech buys more risk reduction than inside other industries, provided the risk model is trained on biotech-specific data.

4 · The breadth is in small caps

Biotech is a sliver of the largest 500 companies but over 20% of the smallest. More than 400 index names, more than any other sector, give a quantitative approach the numbers it needs, and most of them sit where specialists have the biggest edge and the biggest funds cannot go.

5 · Expertise is measurable from public filings

Sort biotech stocks by how heavily specialist funds own them (13F), by the bullishness of insider trades (Form 4), or by short interest, and each spreads returns from the bottom quintile to the top: −2% → +20%, 0% → +15%, and −19% → +16% annualised. These are the three quality signals SAROS carries into every dossier.

6 · The usual factors break; biotech-specific ones work

Traditional momentum (a stock's own trailing return) does nothing, and traditional value (profits to enterprise value) runs backwards because most of the sector has no profits. Redefine them on the sponsor's program: momentum of the closest scientific peers, and value anchored on spending rather than profit, and both spread returns. Blend value, specialist quality and peer momentum and the spread runs from −17% to +21%.

7 · Long and short is what makes it investable

With this many losers, shorting is the least explored and most important tool. A weekly-rebalanced long/short portfolio at 20% target volatility, net of trading, execution and borrow costs, returned 19.1% a year with a 32% maximum drawdown; the XBI did 3.7% with a 64% drawdown over the same 2015–2025 window.

8 · The cycle: winters end

Biotech has lagged the S&P 500 since 2019 and sits in its deepest relative drawdown on record. After the four comparable episodes it returned +63% versus the market over the following two years on average. Meanwhile the supply side is strong: a ~$200B patent cliff by 2030 forces pharma to buy revenue, and approvals have run at or above 50 in five of the last seven years, 72% of 2024's from small and mid-sized companies.

What can go wrong the same paper, read from the other side

Most biotechs lose money

Of the ~1,000 biotechs that ever reached $200M of market cap since 1996, 67% delivered a negative lifetime return; the median company compounded at −15% a year against +1% for the median US company. Half of the losers were acquired at a loss, a tenth were delisted, and the rest are still listed, many below their cash: stranded capital that was never returned.

Following the experts does not remove the risk

The top quintile of specialist ownership beats the XBI, but it is 82% correlated with it: 29% annual volatility for both, and a 61% maximum drawdown against the ETF's 57%. Return, yes; risk reduction, no. That is why argument 7 needs the short book.

The edge is regime-dependent

Specialist-following earns almost all of its excess return in calm markets, a third of the time. In medium and high-volatility months it does no better than the index.

Conventional playbooks invert

Cheap-on-profits is the worst quintile, expensive the best; own-stock momentum is flat to negative. A generalist screen, applied here, selects the wrong side of both. The factor charts in argument 6 are the evidence; the practical consequence is that any model must be rebuilt on biotech-specific definitions before it is trusted.

The drawdown is real and has causes

Since December 2019 the S&P Biotech Index has returned 4.5% a year against 15.1% for the S&P 500, and it is still 21% below its February 2021 peak while the S&P is up 88%. Higher rates choked speculative funding, IRA price-setting cut terminal values, high-profile failures raised the perceived base rate, the 2024 IPO cohort traded ~50% below issue, and, in one banker's words, the generalists are gone. Mean reversion is a tendency, not a schedule.

Data and execution frictions

13F positions arrive 45 days after quarter-end and 72% of specialist exposure sits in large caps, so the cleanest signal is in the least liquid names. Shorting costs, borrow availability and market impact shrink paper spreads. Every readout is binary, registry dates lag real events, and June / December completion dates are placeholders. SAROS shows the coordinates; it does not resolve the coin flips.
